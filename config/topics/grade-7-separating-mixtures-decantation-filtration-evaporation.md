## Methods of separating mixtures

Mixtures can be separated by applying the following methods: decantation, filtration, evaporation, distillation, fractional distillation, sublimation/deposition, chromatography, solvent extraction, crystallization, and using magnets. *(p. 29)*

## Decantation

Sediments can be separated from a liquid by pouring out the liquid. This process is called **decantation**. *(p. 29)*

### Experiment: separating sand from water *(p. 29)*

**Procedure:** Put some sand in a beaker. Add about 200 cm³ of water. Allow the sand to settle. Pour off the water carefully into another beaker.

**Observations:**

- Sand settles at the bottom as sediments.
- Less clean water is poured out.

**Explanation:** Sand does not dissolve in water. Sand is denser than water and thus settles at the bottom as sediment. When poured out, the less dense water flows out.

## Filtration

Decantation leaves suspended particles in the liquid after separation. Filtration is thus improved decantation. **Filtration** is the method of separating insoluble mixtures/particles/solids from a liquid. *(p. 29)*

### Experiment: to separate soil and water using filtration *(pp. 29–30)*

**Procedure:** Fold a filter paper to fit well into a filter funnel. Place the funnel in an empty 250 cm³ beaker. Put one spatula end full of soil into 50 cm³ of water. Stir. Put the soil/water mixture into the filter funnel.

**Observations:**

- Clean water is collected below the filter funnel.
- Soil remains above the filter paper.

**Explanation:** A filter paper is porous and acts like a fine sieve with very small holes. The holes allow smaller water particles to pass through but do not allow bigger soil particles. The liquid which passes through is called the **filtrate**. The solid which does not pass through is called the **residue**.

**Industrial application:** In industries, filtration is used in engine filters to clean up air. *(p. 30)*

## Evaporation *(pp. 30–31)*

**Evaporation** is a method of separating a solute/solid from its solution. This involves heating a solution (solvent and solute) to vaporize the solvent out of the solution mixture, leaving the pure solute/solid. If a mixture contains insoluble solid, it is filtered out first.

### Experiment: to separate a mixture of soil and salt (sodium chloride) *(p. 31)*

**Procedure:**

1. Put one spatula end full of soil on a filter paper.
2. Put one spatula full of common salt/sodium chloride into the same filter paper. Mix well using the spatula.
3. Place about 200 cm³ of water into a beaker.
4. Put the contents of the filter paper into the water. Stir thoroughly using a glass/stirring rod for about one minute.
5. Fold a filter paper into a filter funnel.
6. Pour half the portion of the contents in the beaker into the filter funnel.
7. Put the filtrate into an evaporating dish. Heat on a water bath.

**Observations:**

1. On mixing — colourless crystals and brown soil particles appear on the filter paper.
2. On adding water — common salt dissolves in water; soil particles do not dissolve in water.
3. On filtration — colourless liquid collected as filtrate below the filter funnel/paper; brown residue collected above the filter funnel/paper.
4. On evaporation — colourless crystals collected after evaporation.

**Explanation:**

- The solid mixture of sand and common salt takes the colors of the two.
- On adding water, common salt dissolves to form a solution.
- Soil does not dissolve because it is insoluble in water and thus forms a suspension.
- On filtration, a residue of insoluble soil does not pass through the filter paper — it is collected as residue. Common salt solution is collected as filtrate.
- On heating the filtrate, the solvent/water evaporates/vaporizes out of the evaporating dish leaving common salt crystals.

**Note:** Vaporization/evaporation can take place even without heating. This is the principle/process of drying wet clothes on the hanging line.
