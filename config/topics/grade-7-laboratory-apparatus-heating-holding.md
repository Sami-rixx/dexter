## Apparatus for putting liquids/solids for heating *(p. 19)*

### 1. Test tube

A test tube is a narrow/thin glass apparatus open on one side. The end of the opening is commonly called "the mouth of the test tube".

### 2. Boiling/ignition tube

A boiling/ignition tube is a wider glass apparatus than a test tube, open on one side. The end of the opening is commonly called "the mouth of the boiling/ignition tube".

### 3. Beaker

A beaker is a wide calibrated/graduated lipped glass/plastic apparatus used for transferring liquid solutions which do not normally require very accurate measurements. Beakers are named according to the maximum calibrated/graduated volume they can hold, e.g.:

- "250 ml" beaker — maximum calibrated/graduated volume of 250 millilitres / 250 cubic centimetres
- "1 l" beaker — maximum calibrated/graduated volume of one litre / 1000 cubic centimetres
- "5 l" beaker — the source gives its maximum calibrated/graduated volume as "two litres / 2000 cubic centimetres" *(as printed in the source)*

### 4. Conical flask

A conical flask is a moderately narrow glass apparatus with a wide base and no calibration/graduation. Conical flasks thus carry/hold exact volumes of liquids that have been measured using other apparatus. Some solids can also be put in it. The narrow mouth ensures no spillage. Conical flasks are named according to the maximum volume they can hold, e.g.:

- "250 ml" conical flask — holds a maximum volume of 250 millilitres / 250 cubic centimetres
- "500 ml" conical flask — the source gives its maximum volume as "500 ml / 1000 cubic centimetres" *(as printed in the source)*

### 5. Round bottomed flask

A round bottomed flask is a moderately narrow glass apparatus with a wide round base and no calibration/graduation. It carries/holds exact volumes of liquids measured using other apparatus. The narrow/thin mouth prevents spillage. The flask can also hold (weighed) solids. A round bottomed flask must be held/clamped when in use because of its round base.

### 6. Flat bottomed flask

A flat bottomed flask is a moderately narrow glass apparatus with a wide round base with a small flat bottom. It has no calibration/graduation. Flat bottomed flasks carry/hold exact volumes of liquids measured using other apparatus. The narrow/thin mouth prevents spillage. They can also hold (weighed) solids. A flat bottomed flask must be held/clamped when in use because its narrow base is not stable.

## Apparatus for holding unstable apparatus (during heating) *(p. 20)*

### 1. Tripod stand

A tripod stand is a three-legged metallic apparatus on which unstable apparatus are placed (during heating). Beakers, conical flasks, round bottomed flasks and flat bottomed flasks are placed on top of a tripod stand during heating.

### 2. Wire gauze/mesh

Wire gauze/mesh is a metallic/iron plate of wire crossings. It is placed on top of a tripod stand to:

1. Ensure even distribution of heat to prevent cracking of glass apparatus
2. Hold smaller apparatus that cannot reach the edges of the tripod stand

### 3. Clamp stand

A clamp stand is a metallic apparatus which tightly holds apparatus firmly at their "neck". A clamp stand has a wide metallic base that ensures maximum stability. The height and position of clamping is variable. This requires practice.

### 4. Test tube holder

A test tube holder is a hand-held metallic apparatus which tightly holds a test/boiling/ignition tube firmly at its "neck" on the other end. Some test tube holders have a wooden handle that prevents heat conduction to the hand during heating.

### 5. Pair of tongs

A pair of tongs is a scissor-like hand-held metallic apparatus which tightly holds a small solid sample firmly on the other end.

### 6. Gas jar

A gas jar is a long, wide glass apparatus with a wide base. It is open at one end. It is used to collect/hold gases. This requires practice.

## Apparatus for holding/directing liquid solutions — funnels (to avoid spillage) *(pp. 20–21)*

### 1. Filter funnel

A filter funnel is a wide-mouthed (mainly plastic) apparatus that narrows drastically at the bottom to a long extension. When the long extension is placed on top of another apparatus, a liquid solution can safely be directed through the wide mouth of the filter funnel into the apparatus without spillage. A filter funnel is also used to hold a filter paper during filtration.

### 2. Thistle funnel

A thistle funnel is a wide-mouthed glass apparatus that narrows drastically at the bottom to a very long extension. The long extension is usually drilled through a stopper/cork. A liquid solution can thus be directed into a stoppered container without spillage.

### 3. Dropping funnel

A dropping funnel is a wide-mouthed glass apparatus with a tap that narrows drastically at the bottom to a very long extension. The long extension is usually drilled through a stopper/cork. A liquid solution can thus be directed into a stoppered container without spillage at the rate determined by adjusting the tap.

### 4. Separating funnel

A separating funnel is a wide-mouthed glass apparatus with a tap at the bottom narrow extension. A liquid solution can be directed into a separating funnel without spillage, and it can also safely be removed from the funnel by opening the tap. It is used to separate two or more liquid solution mixtures that form layers/are immiscible. This requires practice.

## Apparatus for heating — burners *(p. 21)*

1. A candle, spirit burner, kerosene stove, charcoal burner/jiko are some apparatus that can be used for heating. Any flammable fuel, when put in a container and ignited, can produce some heat.
2. The **Bunsen burner** is the standard apparatus for heating in a school chemistry laboratory.

## The Bunsen burner

The Bunsen burner was discovered by the German scientist **Robert Wilhelm Bunsen in 1854**. *(p. 21)*

A Bunsen burner uses butane/laboratory gas as the fuel. The butane/laboratory gas is highly flammable and is thus usually stored safely in a secure chamber outside the chemistry school laboratory. It is tapped and distributed into the laboratory through gas pipes. The gas pipes end at the gas tap on a chemistry laboratory bench. If opened, the gas tap releases butane/laboratory gas. Butane/laboratory gas has a characteristic odor/smell that alerts on leakage/an open gas tap. The Bunsen burner is fixed to the gas tap using a strong rubber tube.

### Parts of a Bunsen burner *(pp. 21–22)*

1. **Base plate** — ensures the burner can stand on its own.
2. **Jet** — a hole through which laboratory gas enters the burner.
3. **Collar/sleeve** — an adjustable circular metal attached to the main chimney/barrel with a side hole/entry. It controls the amount of air entering, used during burning.
4. **Air hole** — a hole/entry formed when the collar side hole is in line with the chimney side hole. If the collar side hole is not in line with the chimney side hole, the air hole is said to be "closed"; if the collar side hole is in line with the chimney side hole, the air hole is said to be "open".
5. **Chimney** — a tall round metallic rod attached to the base plate.

### Procedure for lighting/igniting a Bunsen burner *(p. 22)*

1. Adjust the collar to ensure the air holes are closed.
2. Connect the burner to the gas tap using rubber tubing. Ensure the rubber tubing has no side leaks.
3. Turn on the gas tap.
4. Ignite the top of the chimney using a lighted match stick/gas lighter/wooden splint.
5. Do not delay excessively between procedure (iii) and (iv), to prevent highly flammable laboratory gas from escaping/leaking.
