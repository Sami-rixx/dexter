## Sources of electricity *(p. 64)*

Electricity cannot be mined from the ground like coal. So it is called a **secondary source of energy**, meaning that it is derived from **primary sources**, including coal, natural gas, nuclear fission reactions, sunlight, wind, and hydropower. Most direct uses of primary energy are limited to generating heat and motion.

## Electric circuits *(p. 65)*

When there are two or more electrical devices in a circuit with an energy source, there are a couple of basic ways to connect them: in **series** or in **parallel** combinations. A series circuit is a circuit in which two components share a common node and the same current flows through them. However, in a parallel circuit, components share two common nodes.

### Series circuits

A circuit is said to be connected in **series** when the same current flows through all the components in the circuit. In such circuits, the current has only one path. A household decorative string of lights is an example of a series circuit — a series of multiple tiny bulbs connected in series. **If one bulb fuses, all the bulbs in the series do not light up.**

### Parallel circuits

A circuit is said to be **parallel** when the electric current has multiple paths to flow through. The components that are part of the parallel circuits will have a **constant voltage** across all ends.

### Applications of conductors *(p. 67)*

Conductors are quite useful in many ways. They find use in many real-life applications. For example:

- **Mercury** is a common material in thermometers to check the temperature of the body.
- **Aluminium** finds its use in making foils to store food. It is also used in the production of frying pans to store heat quickly.
- **Iron** is a common material used in vehicle engine manufacturing to conduct heat.
- The plate of an iron (box) is made up of **steel** to absorb heat briskly.
- Conductors find their use in **car radiators** to radiate heat away from the engine.

### Applications of insulators *(pp. 67–68)*

As insulators resist the flow of electrons, they find worldwide applications. Some of the common uses include:

- **Thermal insulators** disallow heat from moving from one place to another. Hence, they are used in making thermoplastic bottles. They are also used in fireproofing ceilings and walls.
- **Sound insulators** help in controlling noise levels, as they are good at absorbance of sound. Thus, they are used in buildings and conference halls to make them noise-free.
- **Electrical insulators** hinder the flow of electrons or passage of current through them. So, they are used extensively in circuit boards and high-voltage systems. They are also used in coating electric wires and cables.
