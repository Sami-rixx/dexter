## Acid-base indicators *(p. 42)*

Indicators are useful in identifying substances which look alike. An **acid-base indicator** is a substance used to identify whether another substance is alkaline or acidic. An acid-base indicator works by changing to different colours in neutral, acidic and alkaline solutions/dissolved in water.

## Experiment: to prepare a simple acid-base indicator *(p. 42)*

**Procedure:**

(a) Place some flower petals in a mortar. Crush them using a pestle. Add a little sand to assist in crushing. Add about 5 cm³ of propanone/ethanol and carefully continue grinding. Add another 5 cm³ of propanone/ethanol and continue until there is enough extract in the mortar. Filter the extract into a clean 100 cm³ beaker.

(b) Place 5 cm³ of filtered wood ash, soap solution, ammonia solution, sodium hydroxide, hydrochloric acid, distilled water, sulphuric(VI) acid, sour milk, sodium chloride, toothpaste and calcium hydroxide into separate test tubes.

(c) Put about three drops of the extract in (a) into each test tube in (b). Record the observations made in each case.

### Sample observations *(pp. 42–43)*

| Solution mixture | Colour on adding indicator extract | Nature of solution |
|---|---|---|
| Wood ash | Green | Base/alkaline |
| Soap solution | Green | Basic/alkaline |
| Ammonia solution | Green | Basic/alkaline |
| Sodium hydroxide | Green | Basic/alkaline |
| Hydrochloric acid | Red | Acidic |
| Distilled water | Orange | Neutral |
| Sulphuric(VI) acid | Red | Acidic |
| Sour milk | Green | Basic/alkaline |
| Sodium chloride | Orange | Neutral |
| Toothpaste | Green | Basic/alkaline |
| Calcium hydroxide | Green | Basic/alkaline |
| Lemon juice | Red | Acidic |

**Conclusion** *(p. 43)*: The plant extract is able to differentiate between solutions by their nature — it changes to a similar colour for similar solutions.

1. Since lemon juice is a known acid, then sulphuric(VI) and hydrochloric acids are similar in nature to lemon juice because the indicator shows similar colours — they are acidic in nature.
2. Since sodium hydroxide is a known base/alkali, then the green colour of the indicator shows an alkaline/basic solution.
3. Since pure water is neutral, then the orange colour of the indicator shows neutral solutions.

## The universal indicator and the pH scale *(p. 44)*

The **universal indicator** is a mixture of other indicator dyes. The indicator uses the **pH scale**. The pH scale shows the strength of bases and acids. The pH scale ranges from **1–14**. These numbers are called pH values:

1. pH values 1, 2, 3 show a substance is strongly acidic
2. pH values 4, 5, 6 show a substance is weakly acidic
3. pH value 7 shows a substance is neutral
4. pH values 8, 9, 10, 11 show a substance is a weak base/alkali
5. pH values 12, 13, 14 show a substance is a strong base/alkali

### The pH chart *(p. 44)*

The pH values are determined from a pH chart — a multicoloured paper with each colour corresponding to a pH value:

1. Red corresponds to pH 1, 2, 3 — strongly acidic solutions
2. Orange/yellow corresponds to pH 4, 5, 6 — weakly acidic solutions
3. Green corresponds to pH 7 — neutral solutions
4. Blue corresponds to pH 8, 9, 10, 11 — weakly alkaline solutions
5. Purple/dark blue corresponds to pH 12, 13, 14 — strong alkalis

### Using the universal indicator *(pp. 44–45)*

The universal indicator is available as:

1. Universal indicator paper/pH paper
2. Universal indicator solution

When determining the pH of an unknown solution using:

1. **pH paper** — the pH paper is dipped into the unknown solution. It changes/turns to a certain colour. The new colour is matched/compared to its corresponding one on the pH chart to get the pH value.
2. **Universal indicator solution** — about 3 drops of the universal indicator solution is added to about 5 cm³ of the unknown solution in a test tube. It changes/turns to a certain colour. The new colour is matched/compared to its corresponding one on the pH chart to get the pH value.

### Experiment: to determine the pH value of some solutions *(p. 45)*

**Procedure:**

(a) Place 5 cm³ of filtered wood ash, soap solution, ammonia solution, sodium hydroxide, hydrochloric acid, distilled water, sulphuric(VI) acid, sour milk, sodium chloride, toothpaste and calcium hydroxide into separate test tubes.

(b) Put about three drops of universal indicator solution, or dip a portion of a piece of pH paper, into each. Record the observations made in each case.

(c) Compare the colour in each solution with the colours on the pH chart provided. Determine the pH value of each solution.

## Important notes from the source *(pp. 46)*

1. All the mineral acids — hydrochloric, sulphuric(VI) and nitric(V) acids — are strong acids.
2. Two alkalis/soluble bases, sodium hydroxide and potassium hydroxide, are strong bases/alkalis. Ammonia solution is a weak base/alkali. All other bases are weakly alkaline.
3. Pure/deionized water is a neutral solution.
4. Common salt/sodium chloride is a neutral salt.
5. When an acid and an alkali/base are mixed, the final product has pH 7 and is neutral.
