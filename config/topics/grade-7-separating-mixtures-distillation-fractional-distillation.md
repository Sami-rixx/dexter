## Distillation *(p. 32)*

**Distillation** is an improved evaporation where both the solute and the solvent in the solution are separated/collected. Distillation therefore is the process of separating a solution into its constituent solid solute and the solvent. It involves heating the solution to evaporate/vaporize the solvent out. The solvent vapour is then condensed back to a liquid.

- Salty sea water can be made pure through simple distillation.
- Any mixture with a large difference (about 40 °C) in boiling point can be separated using simple distillation.

## Fractional distillation *(pp. 32–33)*

**Fractional distillation** is an improved simple distillation used specifically to separate **miscible mixtures with very close/near boiling points**.

Fractional distillation involves the following steps:

1. **Heating the mixture** in a conical/round bottomed/flat bottomed flask. The pure substance with the lower boiling point and thus more volatile evaporates/boils/vaporizes first. For example, pure ethanol has a boiling point of 78 °C; pure water has a boiling point of 100 °C at sea level/one atmosphere pressure. When a miscible mixture of ethanol and water is heated, ethanol vaporizes/boils/evaporates first because it is more volatile.
2. **The fractionating column.** The conical/round bottomed/flat bottomed flask is connected to a long glass tube called a **fractionating column**. The purpose of the fractionating column is to offer areas of condensation for the less volatile pure mixture. The fractionating column is packed with **glass beads/broken glass/porcelain/shelves** to increase the surface area of condensation of the less volatile pure mixture.
3. **Condensation on the packing.** When the vapours rise, they condense on the glass beads/broken glass/porcelain/shelves, which become hot. When the temperature of the glass beads/broken glass/porcelain/shelves is beyond the boiling point of the less volatile pure substance, the pure substance rises and condensation takes place on the glass beads/broken glass/porcelain/shelves at a higher level in the fractionating column. The less volatile pure substance trickles/drips back down the fractionating column or back into the flask to be heated again. For example, if the temperature on the glass beads is beyond 78 °C, the more volatile pure ethanol rises to condense on the packing higher in the fractionating column; water condenses and then drips/trickles to the packing lower in the fractionating column because it is less volatile.
4. **The Liebig condenser.** The fractionating column is connected to a **Liebig condenser**, which has a cold water inlet and outlet circulation. The more volatile mixture that reaches the top of the fractionating column is condensed by the Liebig condenser into a receiver. It is collected as the **first fraction**.
5. **Thermometer.** At the top of the fractionating column, a thermometer is placed to note/monitor the temperature of the boiling mixtures. Pure substances have a constant/fixed boiling point. When one component is completely separated, the thermometer reading rises. For example, the thermometer reading remains at 78 °C while ethanol is being separated; when no more ethanol is being separated, the mercury/alcohol level in the thermometer rises.
6. **Subsequent fractions.** The second/subsequent fractions are collected in the receiver after noting a rise in the mercury/alcohol level in the thermometer. For example, the thermometer reading rises to 100 °C when water is being separated. It is passed through the Liebig condenser with the cold water inlet and outlet circulation and collected in a different receiver as the second/subsequent fraction.
7. **Confirming each fraction.** Each fraction collected should be confirmed from known physical/chemical properties/characteristics.

### Example: distinguishing ethanol and water *(p. 33)*

- **Ethanol** is a colourless liquid that has a characteristic smell. When it is put in a watch glass and then ignited, it catches fire and burns with a blue flame.
- **Water** is a colourless liquid that has no smell/odour. When it is put in a watch glass and then ignited, it does not catch fire.

## Industrial applications of fractional distillation *(p. 34)*

On a large scale, fractional distillation is used:

1. **In fractional distillation of crude oil in an oil refinery.** Crude oil is a mixture of many fractions. When heated in a furnace, the different fractions separate out according to their boiling point. In Kenya, fractional distillation takes place at **Changamwe in Mombasa**.
2. **In fractional distillation of air.** Air contains a mixture of three main useful gases which are condensed by cooling to a very low temperature (−200 °C) to form a liquid. The liquid is then heated:
   - **Nitrogen** is the most volatile (−196 °C) and thus comes out as the first fraction.
   - **Argon** (at −186 °C) is the second fraction.
   - **Oxygen** (at −183 °C) is the last fraction.
   - The three gases are very useful industrial gases.
