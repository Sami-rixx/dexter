## What is a magnet? *(p. 71)*

A **magnet** is an object that produces a magnetic field around itself.

Magnets are classified into two groups — **natural and artificial magnets** — based on how they achieved their magnetism, how long they retain their magnetic abilities, and on their magnetic field.

- **Natural magnets** occur in nature and have a weak magnetic field. **Lodestones** are a common example of natural magnets. Natural magnets are much weaker than artificial magnets.
- **Artificial magnets** can be produced by man-made means and have a stronger magnetic field. They can also be shaped as required. When an artificial magnet is shaped in the form of a bar, it is called a **bar magnet**.

## Properties of a bar magnet *(p. 72)*

A bar magnet has properties similar to any permanent magnet:

- It has a **north pole and a south pole** at its two ends. Even if you break a bar magnet from the middle, both pieces will still have a north pole and a south pole, no matter how many pieces you break it into.
- Its magnetic force is **strongest at the poles**.
- If this magnet is suspended freely in the air with a thread, it will not come to rest until the poles are aligned in a **north-south position**. A **Mariner's Compass** uses this property to determine direction.
- If two bar magnets are placed close to each other, their **unlike poles will attract** and **like poles will repel** each other.
- A bar magnet will attract all **ferromagnetic materials** such as iron, nickel and cobalt.

The magnetic force between two moving charges may be described as the effect exerted upon either charge by a magnetic field created by the other.

## Magnetic and non-magnetic materials *(p. 72)*

- **Magnetic substances** are substances that are attracted by a magnet. Examples: iron, cobalt, nickel, etc.
- **Non-magnetic materials** are substances that are not attracted by a magnet. Examples: aluminium, copper, wood, etc.

## Various applications and uses of magnets *(pp. 73–74)*

- **Computers:** we might be using computers in our day-to-day lives but never wonder about the presence of a magnet inside them — magnetic elements on a hard disk help to represent computer data, which is later 'read' by the computer to extract information.
- **TVs, sound speakers and radios:** magnets are used inside them; the small coil of wire and a magnet inside a speaker transform the electronic signal to sound vibrations.
- **Generators and motors:** magnets are used inside a generator to transform mechanical energy into electrical energy; in contrast, other kinds of motors use magnets to change electrical energy to mechanical energy.
- **Cranes:** electrically charged magnets can help cranes to move large metal pieces.
- **Mining/filtering:** magnets are used in filtering machines that separate metallic ores from crushed rocks.
- **Food processing:** magnets are also used in food processing industries for separating small metallic pieces from grains, etc.
- **Medicine:** magnets are used in MRI machines, which are used to create an image of the bone structure, organs, and tissues. Magnets are even used to cure cancer.
- **At home:** you use magnets when you stick a paper on the refrigerator in order to remember something; attaching a magnetic bottle opener to the fridge can come in handy.
- **Compass:** we often use a pocket compass to find directions when we are on a trek — the pocket compass uses a magnetic needle to point north.
- **Cards:** the dark strip on the back of debit and credit cards is magnetic and is used to store data, like computers' hard drives.
- **Collecting nails:** magnets can help collect all the nails which are scattered on the ground after a repair job.
